\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the breeder's equation gets right and wrong}\label{sec:disc_be}

The breeder's equation gets the directions right and the magnitudes wrong, and ex ante valuations of phenotyping tools need the magnitudes. It implies that one year less of a \NumCfgCycleYears-year cycle raises annual gain by \NumCycOneYearPct\%, that accuracy enters only through the square root of heritability, and that accuracy is worth more when heritability is low \citep{Cobb2019Enhancing,Hickey2017Genomic}. Across the explored heritability and genotype-by-environment (GxE) settings, the per-cycle uplifts from the equation and from the simulation were closely correlated (\NumBEcorMin to \NumBEcorMax within each heritability level; Supplementary Section S5). These directions are not findings of this study.

The equation errs in two ways that matter for valuation. First, it overstates the accuracy channel: in the four-stage pipeline under a fixed budget, the simulated accuracy effect was \NumBEratioHtwoTen of the equation's prediction at the central heritability, and the ratio fell from \NumBEratioHtwoFive at $h^2_w$ = \NumCfgRobHtwoLow to \NumBEratioHtwoThirty at $h^2_w$ = \NumCfgRobHtwoHigh (Section~\ref{sec:res_be}). The equation treats each stage as a draw from an unselected pool, whereas selection depletes genetic variance stage by stage, so each later gain in accuracy acts on less variance. Second, it misses how the cost channel depends on GxE (Section~\ref{sec:disc_gxe}). A valuation that plugs a tool's error reduction into the equation would thus nearly double the value of accuracy at the central heritability. Similar gaps between breeder's-equation predictions and stochastic simulation have been reported for shorter cycles in rice \citep{Seck2024Stochastic}, so the same caution applies to the cycle-time channel, which our model passes into annual gain in closed form.

The selection chain shows where the single accuracy term of the equation hides structure. Halving the plot error variance raised selection accuracy by \NumChainAccRelONErrFifty\% in the unreplicated observation nursery but by at most \NumChainAccRelAYTErrFifty\% in the replicated yield trials (Table~\ref{tab:chain}), so accuracy pays most at the early stages, where many lines are discarded on one plot each.

\subsection{Low heritability and strong genotype-by-environment interaction}\label{sec:disc_gxe}

Accuracy and cheaper plots matter more in the target environments that the public trial data describe than in the settings that simulation studies usually assume. The East African multi-environment trials gave a within-environment plot heritability near \NumEmpTZHtwoAll and a GxE-to-genetic variance ratio near \NumEmpTZKappaAll (Table~\ref{tab:params}), so the model used $h^2_w$ = \NumCfgHtwoYield and $\kappa$ = \NumCfgKappa. At these values, the per-cycle uplift of a profile with the evidence-based error reduction of \NumCfgErrRedCentral\% was worth \NumExchMoHtwoTenErrTen months of cycle compression, and a profile with an error reduction of \NumCfgErrRedMax\% was worth \NumExchMoHtwoTenErrFifty months, more than one year (Figure~\ref{fig:fig2_time_beats_accuracy}b). At $h^2_w$ = \NumCfgRobHtwoHigh the same profiles were worth \NumExchMoHtwoThirtyErrTen and \NumExchMoHtwoThirtyErrFifty months, and at $h^2_w$ = \NumCfgRobHtwoLow they were worth \NumExchMoHtwoFiveErrTen and \NumExchMoHtwoFiveErrFifty months. A program that judged a tool at the higher heritability values that simulation studies usually assume would undervalue accuracy more than threefold. The comparison holds only within one definition: the model uses the within-environment plot heritability, $h^2_w = V_g/(V_g+V_e)$, whereas per-trial heritabilities (median \NumEmpTrialHtwoMedTZ in one dataset) include that trial's GxE in the genetic term and are larger.

The cost channel grows with GxE because cheaper plots buy environments, and environments are what strong GxE makes scarce. When the GxE variance is large relative to the genetic variance, extra plots within an environment do little for the heritability of a line mean, whereas extra environments do. Under a fixed budget, entries trade off against replication \citep{Lorenz2013Resource}. The optimizer spends the plots freed by a cheaper method on more environments or more lines, and this reallocation pays most where GxE limits selection. At the central heritability and a cost reduction of \NumBEcostRedMax\%, the simulated uplift rose from \NumBEcostKappaLoSim\% at $\kappa$ = \NumCfgRobKappaLow to \NumBEcostKappaHiSim\% at $\kappa$ = \NumCfgRobKappaHigh, whereas the breeder's equation gave \NumBEcostKappaLoBE\% and \NumBEcostKappaHiBE\%. The equation overstates the cost channel at low GxE and misses most of its rise.

\subsection{What a pilot should measure, and how}\label{sec:disc_pilot}

A pilot should first measure how far a tool shortens the cycle, and second how much it reduces error, against a yardstick that keeps genetic variance intact. At the indifference hurdle, the expected value of perfect information was \NumEVPPIIndiffCyc million USD for compression and \NumEVPPIIndiffErrRed million USD for the error reduction (Figure~\ref{fig:fig5_what_to_measure}c). Compression cannot be read from seconds saved per plot: only a planting or crossing decision that waits for scored and cleaned data can move earlier (Figure~\ref{fig:fig7_cycle_pathway}a). Season-, seed- and regulation-limited waits do not shorten, and in a two-season area the smallest useful step is one season. A pilot cannot observe a full cycle, but it can record, under both workflows, the date on which each stage decision becomes possible and the stages at which data are the bottleneck, and project compression from these records.

Error reduction needs an empirical yardstick, and agreement with manual scores is not one. The stand-covariate analysis supplies one. Adjusting plot yield for harvested stand, centered within genotype, cut plot error variance by a median proportion of \NumStandDeltaEMed (interquartile range \NumStandDeltaELo to \NumStandDeltaEHi) across \NumStandNtrials trials, close to the evidence-based \NumCfgErrRedCentral\% (Supplementary Section S4). The same analysis shows the trap: a naive phenotypic stand adjustment removed a median share of \NumStandDeltaGNaiveMed of the genetic variance, because stand was heritable (median \NumStandHtwoMed) and genetically correlated with yield (median \NumStandRgMed). A tool covariate can thus reduce residual variance while removing the signal that selection needs, so a pilot should estimate genetic and error variance with and without the tool measurement in replicated trials and report the change in heritability and in genotype rankings. Image error can also be genotype dependent and survive replication \citep{Zhou2021Identification}, so validation against an independent reference matters. In the stress block, a tool that added plot error (error reduction set to \NumCfgStressErr\%) changed per-cycle gain by \NumStressUpliftPct\%, and without compression its median value was \NumStressNPVCycZero million USD, positive in only \NumStressPrPosCycZero\% of economic states.

\subsection{Economic context weighs as much as tool performance}\label{sec:disc_context}

Whether a tool pays depends as much on the economy around the program as on the tool. Under the base prior, cycle compression had the largest total Sobol index (\NumSobolSTCyc), the discount rate (\NumSobolSTDisc) and bean price (\NumSobolSTPrice) followed closely, then the adoption ceiling (\NumSobolSTAdopt), whereas error and cost reduction had indices of \NumSobolSTErrRed and \NumSobolSTCostRed (Figure~\ref{fig:fig5_what_to_measure}a). The discount rate ranked first in \NumRvNdiscFirst of the \NumRvNscenarios robustness scenarios, namely those that narrow the prior on compression to at most one year or that lengthen the horizon to \NumCfgHorizonLong years, and compression ranked first in the other \NumRvNcycFirst (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). When compression was restricted to zero or one year with no adoption ramp, slower adoption and a \NumCfgHorizonLong-year horizon, error reduction (\NumRvSTErrConservativeZeroOne) ranked above compression (\NumRvSTCycConservativeZeroOne) among the technical inputs. A pilot cannot learn the discount rate or the bean price; they define the scenarios in which a tool is judged. Discount rates used in practice span a wide range \citep{Harberger2015Musings,Groom2022Future}, and observed adoption of improved bean varieties has been slow \citep{Alene2019Measuring}, so the same tool can be worth very different amounts to different funders. With three years of compression, the median equivalent annual value at the baseline discount rate and adoption ceiling ranged from \NumEAAcycThreePriceLow to \NumEAAcycThreePriceHigh USD per hectare per year across the explored bean prices (Figure~\ref{fig:fig4_value_follows_time}b), so price alone moved the value of the same tool several-fold.

The running cost of the tool sets a floor on the compression it must deliver. At a running cost of \NumCfgRunCostMid million USD per year, breaking even at the baseline economic state required about \NumBECompCostMid years of compression (Table~\ref{tab:netvalue}). A program that cannot remove more than a season from its cycle needs a cheaper tool or one shared across crops and programs.

\subsection{Limitations and generality}\label{sec:disc_limits}

Seven limitations bound these results. First, cycle compression is an assumed scenario input applied in closed form, not a simulated result, and the model passes its full breeder's-equation effect into annual gain without a penalty in genetic variance or accuracy \citep{Seck2024Stochastic,Gorjanc2018Optimal}. At the central heritability, one year of compression matches the per-cycle uplift of the evidence-based profile if \NumPassNeedErrTen\% of that effect is realized, and it matches the profile with \NumCfgErrRedMax\% error reduction only if \NumPassNeedErrFifty\% is realized, more than the full effect. Second, gains are measured over the check varieties that farmers grow. This basis fits a valuation, because economic value accrues against those varieties, but it gives per-cycle uplifts at low heritability about two to three times those on the founder-mean basis used in the breeder's-equation comparison. Third, the model has a single additive yield trait, so it cannot value accuracy for visually scored traits such as disease severity, where rater differences are large \citep{Bock2021Plant}. A smartphone tool does not measure grain yield, and its effect on yield-plot error runs through covariates such as stand.

Fourth, the variance components come from a limited set of public trials (Table~\ref{tab:params}; Supplementary Section S3), and the per-trial estimates scatter widely. Across connected sets and balanced cores of one dataset, the plot heritability ranged from \NumEmpTZHtwoSetMin to \NumEmpTZHtwoSetMax and the variance ratio from \NumEmpTZKappaSetMin to \NumEmpTZKappaSetMax, which the robustness block covers only in part. Fifth, the stand count was taken at harvest, not at emergence, so it includes plant losses that are partly genetic; an early smartphone count would differ in both its error-reducing power and its genetic content. Sixth, the simulation is stochastic with \NumNrepsPerCell replicates per cell, and the minor technical inputs cannot be ranked against one another because their indices lie near the Monte Carlo noise. Seventh, ex ante valuations of research tend toward optimism \citep{Hurley2014Reexamining,Flyvbjerg2021Costbenefit}, so the monetary values rank inputs and scenarios and are not forecasts.

The approach extends beyond beans. Programs with low plot heritability and strong GxE, typical of rainfed smallholder target environments, sit where accuracy is worth most, where the breeder's equation misstates its size and where cheaper plots buy scarce environments. The evaluation chain used here, from tool metrics through program-level gain under a budget to adoption-weighted value and value of information, needs only a program's pipeline, variance components and economic context.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

A breeding program buys gain and value per year and per dollar, not agreement with manual scores. In a four-stage common-bean pipeline calibrated to public East African trials, the breeder's equation got the directions right but overstated the value of accuracy, with a simulated effect of \NumBEratioHtwoTen of its prediction at the central heritability, and it missed how the value of cheaper plots grows with GxE. At the low heritability and strong GxE found in the data, an evidence-based error reduction was worth \NumExchMoHtwoTenErrTen months of cycle compression, and a large one more than a year. Under the base prior, value depended most on the assumed compression, with the discount rate and bean price close behind; when compression was limited to at most one year, the discount rate ranked first. A pilot should therefore first establish which decisions wait for data and how much earlier a tool can make them, and then measure error reduction in replicated trials with a yardstick that keeps genetic variance intact. The evaluation chain from tool metrics to adoption-weighted value and value of information offers programs and funders a reusable basis for judging digital phenotyping investments beyond agreement with manual scores.
