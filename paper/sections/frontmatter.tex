\begin{abstract}
\textbf{CONTEXT:} Phenotyping tools are judged by agreement with manual scores, but breeding programs pay for genetic gain. A tool can act through more accurate measurement, cheaper plots under a fixed budget and shorter cycles; the breeder's equation is the usual shortcut for valuing these channels.

\textbf{OBJECTIVE:} To quantify what each channel adds to gain and value in an East African common-bean program, and what a pilot should measure first.

\textbf{METHODS:} We estimated plot heritability and the ratio of genotype-by-environment to genetic variance from public bean trials. A stochastic simulation of a four-stage pipeline under a fixed budget compared manual and tool phenotyping. Calibrated gain was valued through adoption and discounting and analyzed with global sensitivity and value-of-information methods. Cycle compression was an assumed input.

\textbf{RESULTS AND CONCLUSIONS:} The trials gave a plot heritability near \NumEmpTZHtwoAll and a variance ratio near \NumEmpTZKappaAll. The breeder's equation predicted the directions but overstated the accuracy effect (simulated effect \NumBEratioHtwoTen of its prediction) and missed how the value of cheaper plots depends on genotype-by-environment interaction. Here, an evidence-based error reduction was worth \NumExchMoHtwoTenErrTen months of cycle compression; a large one, over a year. Assumed compression contributed most to the variance of value, followed closely by discount rate and price. Learning the achievable compression was worth more than learning the error reduction.

\textbf{SIGNIFICANCE:} The study offers a reusable chain for valuing digital phenotyping investments, from tool metrics to adoption-weighted value and value of information, and shows that the common shortcut misstates the accuracy channel.
\end{abstract}
