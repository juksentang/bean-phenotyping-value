\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Phenotyping tools are judged by how closely they agree with manual scores and by how fast they run, but a breeding program buys a tool for the genetic gain and economic value it adds per year and per dollar. Phenotyping is widely described as a limiting step in crop breeding \citep{Song2021Highthroughput,Fasoula2020Phenotyping,Kholova2021Pursuit}, and in public programs it still rests largely on visual rating \citep{Walter2015Plant}, which varies between raters \citep{Bock2021Plant,Lasdun2024Participatory}. Image-based and artificial intelligence (AI) tools that could replace such ratings are validated against manual references and by run time \citep{Mochida2018Computer,Liu2021Pocketmaize,McDonald2022Automated,Hu2025Openpheno}. Agreement need not translate into better selection: image traits can lose heritability without losing association power \citep{Gage2018Comparing}, and part of the image error can be genotype dependent, so replication does not remove it \citep{Zhou2021Identification}. A tool can change a program through three channels. It can raise accuracy by reducing measurement error. It can lower the cost per plot, so that a fixed budget buys more plots or more environments. It can shorten the breeding cycle, so that selection decisions come earlier.

The breeder's equation already gives the direction of each channel, but not the magnitudes a program needs. Annual gain is $\Delta G = i\, r\, \sigma_A / L$, the product of selection intensity, selection accuracy and additive genetic standard deviation divided by the years per cycle \citep{Cobb2019Enhancing,Hickey2017Genomic}. By algebra alone, one year less of a \NumCfgCycleYears-year cycle raises annual gain by \NumCycOneYearPct\%, whereas accuracy enters only through the square root of heritability. It follows that time outweighs accuracy when heritability is high, and that accuracy matters more when heritability is low. These directions are not findings. The equation does not say how large the channels are in a multi-stage testing pipeline, where selection depletes genetic variance from stage to stage and a fixed budget trades entries against replicates and environments \citep{Lorenz2013Resource,Gorjanc2018Optimal}. It does not say how the cost channel interacts with genotype-by-environment interaction (GxE), although in a simple simulation higher throughput outweighed lower accuracy when the extra capacity went to more environments \citep{Lane2021High}. It is silent on value, which depends on variety adoption and discounting. Nor does it say which uncertain input a program should resolve before investing. Breeding simulation can answer the two genetic questions \citep{Gaynor2020Alphasimr,Peixoto2023Simulationbased,Jahufer2021Deterministic}, and adoption economics the two economic ones.

Such simulations are only as informative as their genetic parameters, which are usually assumed. Simulation studies of breeding programs typically fix plot heritability and the genetic correlation between environments by assumption \citep{Gorjanc2018Optimal,Lin2023Simulations,Seck2024Stochastic}. East African common-bean programs work in the opposite regime. National yields are low \citep{Beebe2013Phenotyping,Hillocks2006Phaseolus}, regional trials show strong GxE \citep{Amongi2023Gain,Amongi2025Yield}, and the path from a program decision to a widely grown variety rarely takes less than ten years \citep{Kholova2021Pursuit}. Low plot heritability and large GxE change the balance between the three channels, because error that a tool removes is a larger share of the phenotypic variance, and plots spent on more environments can buy more accuracy than plots spent on more replicates. Public multi-environment trial data from these programs now allow the variance components to be estimated rather than assumed.

Economic valuation of phenotyping is scarce, and ex ante valuations of breeding investments tend toward optimism. The only economic model of phenotyping adoption we found is theoretical, and its authors note that information on costs and on the contribution to genetic gain is lacking \citep{Awada2018Adoption}. A costing study of African breeding pipelines recommends digital data capture without valuing it \citep{Das2025Costing}. Ex ante evaluations of breeding programs use adoption paths and net present value (NPV) \citep{Staver2020ExAnte,Grovermann2022Three,Janssen2018Costbenefit}, but reported returns to agricultural research are inflated \citep{Hurley2014Reexamining}, and cost and benefit forecasts for public investments are biased and fat tailed \citep{Flyvbjerg2021Costbenefit}. A credible valuation should therefore show which inputs drive the result and what it would be worth to learn them before investing, which is the domain of global sensitivity analysis \citep{Saltelli2010Variance} and value-of-information analysis \citep{Claxton2001Bayesian,Strong2013Estimating}. To our knowledge, and within the searches described in Supplementary Section S2, no previous study values AI-based phenotyping through a coupled breeding-simulation and adoption-economics model, or applies value-of-information analysis to this decision.

This study values a generic smartphone computer-vision phenotyping tool, described only by its technical attributes, in an East African common-bean breeding program. We couple a stochastic simulation of a four-stage pipeline with a \NumCfgCycleYears-year cycle, in which manual and tool programs work under the same fixed budget (\NumNcellsOK combinations of tool profile and budget), to a model of variety adoption and discounting over \NumCfgHorizon years. We address three questions: how much each channel changes genetic gain when plot heritability and GxE are set from public trial data; how far the breeder's equation misstates these effects in a budget-constrained multi-stage pipeline; and which input drives the value of the tool, and so what a pilot should measure first. The analysis is ex ante and conditional. It reports what a tool with stated properties would be worth in the simulated program, and cycle compression is an assumed scenario input, not a simulated result. The study makes four contributions. It estimates within-environment plot heritability and the ratio of GxE to genetic variance from public East African bean trials to set the model. It quantifies where the breeder's equation overstates the accuracy channel and misses how strongly the cost channel grows with GxE. It expresses measurement-error reduction in months of equivalent cycle compression. It ranks the economic and technical inputs by global sensitivity and by the expected value of partial perfect information. Together these steps form a reusable evaluation chain for digital phenotyping investments: from tool metrics, to program-level gain under a fixed budget, to calibrated gain, to adoption-weighted value, to attribution and value of information. The chain should transfer to other programs with low plot heritability and strong GxE, as is typical of smallholder target environments.
