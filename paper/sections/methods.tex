\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Study design and conceptual framework}
\label{sec:design}

The study links tool metrics to investment value in five steps (Figure~\ref{fig:fig1_design}). A tool profile, defined by five technical inputs, is simulated in a stochastic four-stage breeding program under a fixed budget (Layer 1), with genetic parameters from public East African trial data. The simulated genetic gain over the check varieties is calibrated to realized progress in common bean and converted into adoption-weighted value over a grid of economic states (Layer 2). The resulting net present value (NPV) is attributed to its inputs and entered into a value-of-information analysis. The tool enters only through its technical inputs, so the results apply to any smartphone computer-vision tool with these properties (Supplementary Section S1 reviews their field performance). Throughout, ``accuracy'' denotes the inverse of the error variance of the selection phenotype, not the selection accuracy of the breeder's equation.

\label{fig:fig1_design}
\end{figure}

A tool can raise annual genetic gain through three channels (Figure~\ref{fig:fig7_cycle_pathway}). More accurate measurement lowers plot error and so raises selection accuracy. A lower cost per plot lets a fixed budget buy more lines or environments. Faster scoring and analysis can shorten the cycle, but only where the next planting or crossing decision waits for the data. Annual gain is per-cycle gain divided by the cycle length, so accuracy and cost act on the numerator and cycle time on the denominator.

\subsection{Public trial data and variance components}
\label{sec:data}

The genetic parameters of the simulation were estimated from three public datasets of common-bean yield trials in East Africa. TSEZVG (Harvard Dataverse) holds plot-level grain yield from alpha-lattice trials of bush and climbing beans in Uganda and Tanzania \citep{Mukankusi2022TSEZVG,Saradadevi2021Multivariate}. ZUDPEP (Harvard Dataverse) holds plot-level yield from preliminary yield trials of eight trial series of a Ugandan bean breeding program, each repeated across sites in Uganda and Tanzania and across seasons \citep{Mukankusi2021ZUDPEP,Amongi2019Yield}. Dryad dz08kpsbz gives single-trial variance components from published analyses of variance, without plot data \citep{Amongi2026Dryad,Amongi2026Genomics}. We also recomputed the same quantities from the variance components reported for regional and national trials by \citet{Amongi2023Gain} and \citet{Amongi2025Yield}. Data cleaning, the grouping of trials into connected sets and all estimates by dataset are in Supplementary Section S3.

Variance components were estimated with linear mixed models fitted by restricted maximum likelihood in the R packages lme4 and glmmTMB \citep{Bates2015Fitting,Brooks2017glmmTMB}. For each connected set of trials, yield was modeled with a fixed environment effect and random effects for genotype, genotype by environment, replicate within environment and incomplete block within replicate. The residual variance was allowed to differ between environments, and the plot error variance $V_e$ is the mean of the environment residual variances. Homogeneous-residual fits served as a check. Confidence intervals are percentiles of a parametric bootstrap that simulated from, and refitted, the same model.

Three heritability definitions are kept apart. The model's parameter is the within-environment plot heritability $h^2_w = V_g/(V_g+V_e)$, where $V_g$ is the genetic variance across environments. The GxE-to-genetic variance ratio is $\kappa = V_{ge}/V_g$, with $V_{ge}$ the genotype-by-environment variance, so that the genetic correlation between environments is $1/(1+\kappa)$. A per-trial heritability absorbs that trial's genotype-by-environment variance into its genetic variance, so it estimates $(V_g+V_{ge})/(V_g+V_{ge}+V_e)$ and exceeds $h^2_w$. The total heritability $h^2_{\mathrm{total}} = V_g/(V_g+V_{ge}+V_e)$ is lower than $h^2_w$. Values are never compared across definitions. The estimates, and the values of $h^2_w$ and $\kappa$ that the model uses, are reported in Section~\ref{sec:res_params} and Table~\ref{tab:params}.

A stand-covariate analysis gives an empirical scale for a realistic error reduction, using a trait that smartphone tools already count (Supplementary Section S4). In each ZUDPEP trial, area-based plot yield was fitted with and without a stand covariate (linear, quadratic or logarithmic, chosen by the Akaike information criterion). Stand was centered within genotype, so that only its non-genetic variation was regressed out. The error reduction is the relative fall in residual variance between the two fits. For comparison, we fitted a naive phenotypic adjustment and measured the share of genetic variance it removed, with the heritability of stand and its genetic correlation with yield. TSEZVG has no stand record, and the ZUDPEP count was taken at harvest, not at emergence.

\subsection{Breeding simulation}
\label{sec:layer1}

The case is a public common-bean program in East Africa with a four-stage testing pipeline and a \NumCfgCycleYears{}-year cycle from cross to release: an observation nursery (ON), a preliminary yield trial (PYT), an advanced yield trial (AYT) and a national performance trial (NPT). The cycle length is an assumption; a dry-bean simulation study used eight years \citep{Lin2023Simulations}, and the path from a program decision to a widely grown variety, adoption included, rarely takes less than ten \citep{Kholova2021Pursuit}. Each cycle starts with \NumCfgNcrosses{} crosses among founders, each with \NumCfgNprogeny{} F1 plants, followed by \NumCfgNssd{} generations of single-seed descent to F5. The NPT selects \NumCfgNreleased{} lines for release. Stage sizes are assumptions in the style of regional bean breeding networks in Africa (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}). Yield is a single additive polygenic trait on the \NumCfgNchr{} chromosomes of the common-bean genome \citep{Schmutz2014Reference}, with \NumCfgNfounders{} founders, \NumCfgSegSites{} segregating sites and \NumCfgQTLperChr{} quantitative trait loci per chromosome. Mean yield is \NumCfgMeanYield{} kg/ha, within the range of national average yields reported for African bean-growing countries \citep{Beebe2013Phenotyping}. The additive genetic standard deviation of \NumCfgSigmaA{} kg/ha is an assumption whose scale the calibration removes (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

Each cycle was simulated in AlphaSimR \citep{Gaynor2020Alphasimr}, which is widely used to compare breeding-program designs in replicated stochastic runs \citep{Gorjanc2018Optimal,FritscheNeto2023Improving,Seck2024Stochastic}. Every replicate runs a manual and a tool program from the same founder population and the same pipeline seed, so crosses and generation advance are identical and noise largely cancels in the paired difference. Each stage applies truncation selection on one phenotype per line. Let $\sigma^2_A$ be the genetic variance of the selection target and let the genotype-by-environment variance be $\sigma^2_{GE} = \kappa\,\sigma^2_A$. The heritability of a line mean over $r$ replicates and $e$ environments is then
\begin{equation}
h^2_{\mathrm{eff}} = \left[\, 1 + \frac{\kappa}{e} + \frac{1-h^2}{h^2\, r\, e} \,\right]^{-1},
\label{eq:heff}
\end{equation}
where $h^2$ is the plot heritability $h^2_w$ of Section~\ref{sec:data}. GxE thus enters only through $\kappa$, and its weight falls with the number of environments but not with the number of replicates. The error variance of the single phenotype is
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2_{\varepsilon} = V_{\mathrm{ref}} \left( \frac{1}{h^2_{\mathrm{eff}}} - 1 \right),
\label{eq:vare}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the genetic variance of the F5 pool entering the ON. Plot error therefore stays constant across stages while selection depletes genetic variance, so realized heritability falls from stage to stage. The tool's error reduction $\delta$ scales the single-plot error variance by $(1-\delta)$ before averaging. With that variance normalized to one, $\sigma^2_A = h^2/(1-h^2)$, and the tool program replaces $h^2$ in Equation~(\ref{eq:heff}) by
\begin{equation}
h^2_{\mathrm{tool}} = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{\sigma^2_A + (1-\delta)}.
\label{eq:htool}
\end{equation}
The tool leaves the GxE term unchanged, and $\delta$ is negative for a tool noisier than manual scoring.

Both programs spend the same budget per cycle. Trial cost is the sum over stages of lines $\times$ environments $\times$ replicates $\times$ cost per plot, plus a fixed cost per environment and stage, plus the tool fixed cost, which is zero for the manual program. The cost per environment (site establishment, travel, trial management) is paid by both programs, is not reduced by the tool and is zero in the main analysis. The tool's cost per plot is the manual cost (USD \NumCfgCostPerPlot) times $(1-\text{cost reduction})$. A grid search varies PYT size from \NumCfgPYTmin{} to \NumCfgPYTmax{} lines and PYT environments from one to \NumCfgPYTenvMax, with the ON size tied to the PYT size and the AYT and NPT sizes fixed. Among the allocations within budget, the search picks the maximizer of the breeder's-equation approximation of annual gain \citep{Cobb2019Enhancing}, $\hat{G} = L^{-1}\sum_{s} i_s \sqrt{h^2_{\mathrm{eff},s}}\,\sigma_A$, where $i_s$ is the selection intensity of stage $s$. It thereby trades entries against replication \citep{Lorenz2013Resource}. The allocation is chosen once per program and profile. Supplementary Section S7 gives the optimizer grid and a stage-by-stage trace of the selection chain (Table~\ref{tab:chain}), which holds the manual allocation fixed to isolate the accuracy channel.

Genetic gain per cycle is the mean true genetic value of the released lines minus that of the check varieties, taken as the top founders in the same number as the released lines (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). A tool's advantage is the per-cycle uplift $u = 100\,\bar{D}/\bar{G}_{\mathrm{man}}$, the mean paired difference over the mean manual gain. Cycle compression is an assumed scenario input applied in closed form. The simulation does not derive it, and it leaves selection unchanged. Annual gain is the per-cycle gain divided by $L$ for the manual program and by $L-c$ for the tool program with compression $c$. The annual uplift is $100\,[(1+u/100)\,L/(L-c) - 1]$, and the equivalent compression, $L - L/(1+u/100)$, gives the years of compression that raise annual gain by as much as $u$.

The five technical inputs are measurement-error reduction, whole-plot cost reduction, cycle compression, tool fixed cost and program budget (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}). The simulated trait is yield, which a smartphone tool does not measure directly. Error reduction is therefore an effective reduction in the error variance of the selection phenotype, for example through covariate adjustment with image-derived stand counts (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The evidence-based profile has an error reduction of \NumCfgErrRedCentral\%, a cost reduction of \NumCfgCostRedCentral\% and a compression of \NumCfgCycCompCentral{} year. We found no study that measured how far a phenotyping tool shortens a breeding cycle, although precedents exist for other cycle-shortening technologies \citep{Lin2023Simulations,Chaingeni2025More,Katiyar2026Unified}. Because the budget grid lies above the operating cost of single national programs (USD \NumNatCostMin{} to \NumNatCostMax{} per cycle; \citealp{Das2025Costing,Katiyar2026Unified}), the pipeline represents a regional network, and bean area is summed over the same countries.

\subsection{Calibration of simulated gain}
\label{sec:calibration}

Gain is measured over the check varieties, taken as the best founders, because they stand in for the elite varieties that farmers already grow. Economic value accrues only as far as a new variety outyields the variety it replaces, so this basis matches Layer 2. Gain over the founder mean is kept as a secondary output. Before calibration, the manual program gains on average \NumDGvsBaseTradPerCycle{} kg/ha per cycle over the founder mean and \NumDGtradPerCycle{} kg/ha over the checks. Relative uplifts are therefore larger on the check basis, because the manual gain that divides them is smaller. The breeder's-equation comparison (Section~\ref{sec:becheck}) uses the founder-mean basis, which is the basis of that equation.

Simulated gain was rescaled by one common factor so that the manual program's mean annual gain equals realized progress in common-bean breeding. Before calibration, the manual program gains \NumDGtradPerYear{} kg/ha per year over the checks, or \NumDGtradPctPerYearRaw\% of mean yield. The target is \NumGainTargetPct\% per year, the pooled realized gain of common-bean programs in MA1 (Supplementary Section S2). East African estimates alone are of similar size \citep{Amongi2023Gain}, and public programs that serve smallholders realize well under one percent annually \citep{Cobb2019Enhancing}. The factor of \NumGainScale{} multiplies the manual gain, the tool gain and the paired difference of every profile, which gives a calibrated manual gain of \NumDGtradPerYearCal{} kg/ha per year and leaves relative uplifts unchanged. Because gain is proportional to $\sigma_A$ in the breeder's-equation approximation, the factor is equivalent to rescaling the assumed $\sigma_A$. A calibration through heritability or $\kappa$ would instead change relative uplifts, and it could not close the gap: across the $h^2_w \times \kappa$ block the manual program gains \NumRobManualGainPctMin{} to \NumRobManualGainPctMax{} percent of mean yield per year, about \NumRobManualGainFoldMin{} or more times the target. Targets of \NumCfgTargetLow{} and \NumCfgTargetHigh\% per year are tested as scenarios (Section~\ref{sec:robustdesign}).

Three meta-analyses of published studies supplied the ranges and central values of the inputs and the calibration target. They pool realized genetic gain in common bean (MA1), agreement between image-based or AI tools and manual scores (MA2), and time and cost savings of digital phenotyping (MA3). Because a tool-manual correlation does not identify error reduction, the central error reduction comes from heritability pairs of image and manual traits, toward the favorable end of the three routes we used to bound it. Searches, inclusion rules, models and all pooled estimates are in Supplementary Section S2.

\subsection{From genetic gain to adoption-weighted value}
\label{sec:layer2}

The value of the tool is the NPV of its incremental cash flow over the manual program across a \NumCfgHorizon{}-year horizon $H$. Let $L_j$ be the cycle length of program $j$, with $L_{\mathrm{man}} = L$ and $L_{\mathrm{tool}} = L-c$. Each program spends its budget evenly over the $L_j$ years of its cycle, so a shorter cycle spends it sooner. The tool fixed cost acts through the Layer 1 allocation, not as an extra cash flow. Release occurs in year $L_j$. From release, the yield advantage over the checks builds at the annual gain $g_j = G_j/L_j$ until it equals the per-cycle gain $G_j$ after $L_j$ years, and then stays at $G_j$. This ramp stands for the successive releases of overlapping cycles. The benefit of program $j$ in year $t \geq L_j$ is
\begin{equation}
B^{j}_t = g_j\,\min(t-L_j+1,\,L_j)\; p\; a(t-L_j)\; A,
\label{eq:benefit}
\end{equation}
where $p$ is the bean price, $a(\cdot)$ the adoption rate and $A$ the bean area. Spending $C^{j}_t$ equals the budget divided by $L_j$ before release and zero afterwards. With $r$ the discount rate,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NPV} = \sum_{t=0}^{H} \frac{\left(B^{\mathrm{tool}}_t - B^{\mathrm{man}}_t\right) - \left(C^{\mathrm{tool}}_t - C^{\mathrm{man}}_t\right)}{(1+r)^{t}}.
\label{eq:npv}
\end{equation}
We use NPV rather than a benefit-cost ratio, which favors underinvestment \citep{Janssen2018Costbenefit}. Adoption follows a logistic curve with a ceiling, a common form in evaluations of returns to breeding research \citep{Grovermann2022Three},
\begin{equation}
a(\tau) = \frac{K}{1 + \exp\left[-k\,(\tau - \tau_{\mathrm{mid}})\right]},
\label{eq:logistic}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the time since release, $K$ the ceiling, $k$ the rate and $\tau_{\mathrm{mid}}$ the midpoint. The ceiling is swept, and the rate and midpoint are fixed in the main analysis (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}). The assumed rate implies faster area gain than the roughly one percentage point per year observed for improved beans \citep{Alene2019Measuring}, so slower rates are tested.

Each tool profile was valued at every state of a full factorial of five levels of each of four economic inputs: bean price, discount rate, adoption ceiling and bean area (\NumNlookupRows{} profile-state combinations). The area levels bracket the dry-bean area harvested in \NumFaoYearFrom--\NumFaoYearTo, \NumFaoSixMin--\NumFaoSixMax{} million ha summed over Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi and Ethiopia \citep{FAO2025FAOSTATQCL}. The baseline state is the grid point nearest the central values (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}). The equivalent annual value (EAV) per hectare converts NPV into a level annual stream,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EAV} = \frac{\mathrm{NPV}\; r}{\left[1-(1+r)^{-H}\right] A}.
\label{eq:eav}
\end{equation}
EAV times area is the level annual cost that the program could carry before NPV falls to zero, the break-even running cost.

\subsection{Global sensitivity and value-of-information analysis}
\label{sec:gsa}

The Layer 1 profiles and the economic levels form a full factorial grid. With area fixed at the baseline, NPV is a table over eight inputs (the five technical inputs plus price, discount rate and adoption ceiling), and every conditional expectation $\mathrm{E}[Y \mid X_S]$ of NPV $Y$ given a subset $S$ of inputs follows by exact averaging over the table (\NumNexactGrid{} values). All inputs take their simulated levels with equal probability, so one prior serves the variance attribution and the decision analysis. First-order and total-order Sobol indices \citep{Sobol2001Global} follow from $S_i = \mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{E}[Y \mid X_i])/\mathrm{Var}(Y)$ and $S^T_i = 1 - \mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{E}[Y \mid X_{-i}])/\mathrm{Var}(Y)$. They carry no sampling error. To gauge the effect of Layer 1 noise, we added normal noise with the standard error of each profile's paired difference, recomputed the indices \NumBootB{} times and report the largest shift of any total-order index. Indices were also computed with compression fixed at zero and for NPV truncated at each year of the horizon.

Two further measures test whether the ranking depends on variance attribution or input independence. Shapley effects \citep{Owen2017Shapley,Song2016Shapley,Iooss2019Shapley} were computed exactly over all orderings of the eight inputs, for independent inputs and for a Gaussian copula discretized on the grid. The copula correlations are assumptions (Supplementary Section S7). PAWN \citep{Pianosi2015Simple} is the median Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between the conditional and unconditional distributions of NPV, computed exactly on the grid, and its magnitude depends on the summary statistic \citep{Puy2020Sensitivity}. Because each measure answers a different question, we compare rankings, not magnitudes. An active-subspace check is in Supplementary Section S7.

The expected value of perfect partial information (EVPPI) measures what learning an input would be worth to a decision between investing in the tool and an alternative with a fixed payoff $\tau$, the hurdle \citep{Claxton2001Bayesian}. For input $X_i$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EVPPI}_i(\tau) = \mathrm{E}_{X_i}\!\left[\max\left(\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{NPV}\mid X_i],\,\tau\right)\right] - \max\left(\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{NPV}],\,\tau\right).
\label{eq:evppi}
\end{equation}
We computed it exactly on the grid by conditioning on each level of each input. The main hurdle is the indifference hurdle $\tau = \mathrm{E}[\mathrm{NPV}]$ (\NumEVprior{} million USD), where EVPPI peaks by construction. Hurdles from zero to \NumCfgHurdleMaxMult{} times that value, and a zero hurdle on NPV net of running costs, are in Supplementary Section S7. Conditioning on grid levels avoids the upward bias of nested Monte Carlo with small inner samples \citep{Brennan2007Calculating} and the regression step of single-sample estimators \citep{Strong2013Estimating,Heath2017Review}. Expected NPV was also split into a pessimistic corner (low price, adoption and compression, high discount rate), the mirror optimistic corner and the remaining states.

\subsection{Breeder's equation versus simulation}
\label{sec:becheck}

To test whether the breeder's equation can stand in for the simulation, we predicted the per-cycle uplift of every profile of the $h^2_w \times \kappa$ block (Section~\ref{sec:robustdesign}) with the multi-stage approximation $\sum_s i_s \sqrt{h^2_{\mathrm{eff},s}}$ that drives the allocation search. Two predictions were made. One, be\_fixed, applies the tool's error reduction to the manual program's optimal allocation, so it contains the accuracy channel only. The other, be\_opt, re-optimizes both programs under the budget with the full tool profile, so it also contains the cost channel. Simulated uplifts were averaged over compression and expressed on the founder-mean basis (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). The simulated accuracy effect is the uplift at a given error reduction minus that at zero error reduction and the same cost reduction. We report its ratio to be\_fixed within each $h^2_w$, averaged over error reductions of at least \NumBEerrMin\%, the correlation of be\_opt with the simulated uplift within each $h^2_w$, and the cost channel at zero error reduction and the largest cost reduction (\NumBEcostRedMax\%) across $\kappa$. Supplementary Section S5 gives the full comparison.

\subsection{Robustness analyses}
\label{sec:robustdesign}

The robustness scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:robust}) re-evaluate the exact NPV table under one changed assumption each, without new Layer 1 simulation; with the base case they number \NumRvNscenarios. Four change the prior on cycle compression: zero or one year with equal weight; zero with probability \NumCfgZeroMassA{} and one to three years sharing the rest; zero with probability \NumCfgZeroMassB{} and one year with the rest; and half-year steps (zero, \NumCfgSeasonMid{} and one year), which reflect the two bean seasons of bimodal-rainfall areas \citep{Katungi2018Effect,Jha2023Characterizing}, with a matching reference on the same time step. Seven change the economic structure: no ramp (the full per-cycle gain applies from release); recurrent cycles, in which the advantage keeps accruing and spending continues; a \NumCfgHorizonLong{}-year horizon; adoption rates of \NumCfgKLow{} and \NumCfgKMid; a conservative combination of no ramp, a rate of \NumCfgKLow{} and the longer horizon; and the same combination with compression restricted to zero or one year (ConservativeZeroOne). Two change the calibration target, and three subtract a running cost of \NumCfgRunCostLow, \NumCfgRunCostMid{} or \NumCfgRunCostHigh{} million USD in every year of the horizon. The break-even compression at each running cost is interpolated between integer compression levels.

Three Layer 1 blocks test the genetic and cost assumptions. The $h^2_w \times \kappa$ block (\NumNcellsRobust{} profiles) crosses $h^2_w$ of \NumCfgRobHtwoLevels{} and $\kappa$ of \NumCfgRobKappaLevels{} with five levels of error reduction, three of cost reduction and four of compression, at the central fixed cost and budget. It gives the exchange rate between accuracy and cycle time at each $h^2_w$ and feeds the breeder's-equation comparison. The environment-cost block (\NumNcellsEnvCost{} profiles) repeats this tool grid at the central $h^2_w$ and $\kappa$ with a cost per environment and stage of USD \NumCfgEnvCostLevels. For each level we fitted least-squares slopes of the per-cycle uplift on error reduction and cost reduction, expressed per 10-point reduction. The stress block (\NumNcellsStress{} profiles) sets the error reduction to \NumCfgStressErr\%, a tool noisier than manual scoring, at two cost reductions, the central fixed cost and three budgets, with no compression; because compression leaves selection unchanged, its gains apply at every compression level. None of the blocks enters the main lookup table. Supplementary Section S6 gives their design and further results.

Layer 1 ran in R \NumRVersionHPC{} with AlphaSimR \NumAlphaSimRVersionHPC, and all later steps in R \NumRVersionLocal; a scripted chain regenerates every number in the text (Supplementary Section S7). Code and data are archived on Zenodo (Data availability).
